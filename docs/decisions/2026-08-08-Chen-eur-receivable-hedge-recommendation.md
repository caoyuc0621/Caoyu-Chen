# Hedge Recommendation — EUR 4.5 Million Receivable

**To:** Chief Financial Officer  
**From:** Caoyu Chen  
**Date:** 2026-08-08  
**Subject:** Recommendation for the EUR 4.5 million receivable due in 365 days

## A. Exposure Summary

The company expects to collect **EUR 4,500,000 in 365 days**. Because the receivable is denominated in euros but the company evaluates cash flow in U.S. dollars, a decline in EUR/USD would reduce the dollar value of the payment. The Stage 4 market data uses a current EUR/USD spot rate of **1.1535**, a one-year USD rate of **4.01%**, and a one-year EUR proxy rate of **2.678%**. These rates imply a one-year forward of approximately **1.168666 USD/EUR**.

The decision is therefore not whether the company has currency exposure; it does. The decision is how much certainty to buy and how much potential euro appreciation to retain.

## B. Hedge Outcomes

The **unhedged** position provides the most direct participation in currency movements. At the current spot rate, the receivable is worth approximately **$5,190,750.00**. If the euro weakens 5% from today's spot, the proceeds fall to about **$4,931,212.50**. If the euro strengthens 5%, proceeds rise to about **$5,450,287.50**. This flexibility comes with full downside exposure and no budget certainty.

The **forward hedge** locks approximately **$5,258,998.01** for the full receivable. That amount does not depend on the settlement spot rate. Relative to converting the receivable at today's spot, the forward locks roughly **$68,248.01** more in expected dollar proceeds. More importantly, it removes the possibility that a weaker euro creates a shortfall against the budget.

The **money-market hedge** produces essentially the same proceeds, approximately **$5,258,998.01**, because the forward rate is based on covered interest parity. Economically it is a valid substitute for the forward. Operationally, however, it requires borrowing euros today, converting those euros to dollars, investing the dollars, and then using the eventual receivable to repay the euro borrowing. Unless there is a financing or credit reason to prefer this structure, it adds execution steps without materially improving the outcome.

The **purchased put** creates downside protection while preserving upside. The 1.1500 strike establishes a floor, but the premium is **$90,000.00**. At the base spot, the put produces approximately **$5,100,750.00**, which is about **$158,248.01 less** than the forward. If the euro strengthens 5%, however, the put rises to approximately **$5,360,287.50**, exceeding the forward by about **$101,289.49**. The option therefore buys genuine upside participation, but the firm pays for that flexibility up front.

The **sold call** collects a **$45,000.00** premium and caps the effective conversion rate at 1.2000. It should not be viewed as a complete downside hedge on its own because the proceeds still decline when the euro weakens. Its main economic role is as a funding component for a collar.

The **collar** combines the 1.1500 floor and 1.2000 cap and has a net premium cost of **$45,000.00**. At the base spot it produces approximately **$5,145,750.00**. At a 5% stronger euro it reaches approximately **$5,355,000.00**, but the call cap limits further upside. It reduces the option premium compared with the put, but that savings is purchased by surrendering gains above the call strike.

## C. Sensitivity Interpretation

The sensitivity results separate the strategies into two groups.

The forward and money-market hedge provide **certainty**. Their proceeds remain around **$5.259 million** whether the euro falls 5%, stays near today's level, or rises 5%. This is valuable when the receivable is tied to a budget, debt payment, capital commitment, or other fixed-dollar requirement.

The unhedged position and options provide **flexibility**. When the euro weakens 5%, the unhedged position falls to about **$4.931 million**, while the purchased put holds approximately **$5.085 million** and the collar approximately **$5.130 million**. The forward still produces about **$5.259 million**, making it the strongest downside result in this range.

When the euro strengthens 5%, the ranking changes. The unhedged position reaches about **$5.450 million**, the sold-call result about **$5.445 million**, the put about **$5.360 million**, and the collar about **$5.355 million**, all above the forward. That upside is the opportunity cost of locking the rate today.

The key threshold for the put is approximately **1.188666 USD/EUR**. Above that settlement rate, the put's retained appreciation becomes valuable enough to overcome its $90,000 premium and beat the forward. This threshold is roughly **3.05% above today's spot**. Management should therefore choose the put only if preserving upside is worth paying for and there is a credible reason to expect appreciation of that magnitude.

## D. Recommendation

I recommend a **full forward hedge of the EUR 4,500,000 receivable**.

The recommendation is based on the firm's exposure rather than a forecast of the euro. The forward converts an uncertain foreign-currency receipt into approximately **$5,258,998.01 of known dollar cash flow**. It protects against the 5% downside scenario, produces the strongest or essentially strongest result in the weak-euro and base scenarios, and does not require the $90,000 premium of the purchased put.

The money-market hedge provides the same economics but with more transactions. The put is attractive only if management deliberately wants to retain euro upside and is comfortable paying the premium for that possibility. The collar lowers the option cost but gives up some of the upside that makes an option strategy attractive in the first place.

If management's risk appetite is materially more bullish on the euro than assumed here, a partial hedge could be considered—for example, hedging the budget-critical portion with a forward and leaving a smaller portion protected by a put. The model, however, does not provide evidence that such a view should override the basic objective of protecting the receivable's dollar value. For the current decision, the full forward is the cleaner choice.

## E. Executive Justification

The forward best supports **cash-flow stability and budget certainty**. It locks the dollar proceeds today and eliminates the need to manage future exchange-rate movements over the next year. That certainty is especially valuable because a receivable is already an operating exposure; the hedge should reduce volatility rather than create a new directional position.

The forward also has an advantage in **liquidity and implementation**. Unlike the money-market hedge, it does not require immediate euro borrowing and dollar investing. Unlike the put, it does not require a stated premium payment. The model excludes transaction costs and collateral requirements, so actual execution terms should still be reviewed, but the forward remains the most straightforward instrument in the modeled set.

The cost of this recommendation is **foregone upside**. If the euro appreciates substantially, the company will still receive the locked $5.259 million rather than the higher unhedged or option-supported amount. That is a deliberate trade: the company gives up speculative upside in exchange for protecting the cash flow that the business already earned.

For this receivable, that trade is justified. The forward turns a market-dependent cash inflow into a known dollar amount, is supported by both the workbook and independent hand verification, and aligns most directly with a conservative treasury objective.
