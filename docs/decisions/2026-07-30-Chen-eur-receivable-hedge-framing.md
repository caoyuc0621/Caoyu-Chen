Executive Memo

Chief Financial Officer

Our company expects to receive “EUR 4,500,000 in one year”, creating a foreign exchange exposure because the euro amount is fixed while the U.S. dollar value will depend on the EUR/USD exchange rate at settlement. If the euro weakens before payment is received, the company will collect fewer U.S. dollars than anticipated, reducing revenue and making budgeting and cash flow planning less predictable. For example, if the exchange rate declines from 1.10 to 1.00 USD per euro, the expected proceeds would decrease by around “$450,000”. This potential loss demonstrates why managing exchange rate risk should be considered before the receivable is collected.

The three primary hedging approaches are available. A forward contract locks in an exchange rate today, providing certainty regarding the future dollar value of the receivable. Its disadvantage is that the company cannot benefit if the euro appreciates before settlement. A money market hedge achieves a similar result by borrowing and investing funds to effectively lock in today's exchange rate. Although it provides predictable cash flows, it requires financing and is operationally more complex. A currency option establishes a minimum exchange rate while preserving the opportunity to benefit if the euro strengthens. The trade-off is the upfront option premium, which increases the cost of protection.

I recommend proceeding with the next phases of this project before selecting a hedging strategy. Phase 2 will define the analytical framework and required calculations. Phase 3 will build and audit the financial model to ensure accuracy. Phase 4 will replace placeholder assumptions with current market data, and Phase 5 will validate the results and present a final recommendation supported by quantitative analysis. This approach will allow management to compare the available hedging strategies objectively and select the alternative that best balances risk reduction, flexibility, and cost.
